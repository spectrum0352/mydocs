# Immediate actions after cyber attack

This guide provides a comprehensive framework for responding to a cyberattack, merging immediate technical containment with long-term strategic recovery. It is designed to minimize damage within the critical first 30-day window.

Phase 1: Immediate Actions (Minutes to Hours)

The primary goal is to halt the spread of the attack and preserve data integrity.

Alert IT and Management: Employees must immediately report irregularities or suspicious activity.
Isolate Compromised Assets: * Disconnect infected devices from the network immediately.
Isolate the entire network if necessary to prevent lateral movement.
Verify Backups: Examine cloud and local backups for integrity. Ensure they are uninfected and viable for restoration.
Activate Security Systems: Deploy monitoring tools to track malicious activity and ensure the threat is under control.
Employee Awareness: Promptly inform staff of the threat. Provide clear guidance (e.g., "Do not click links" or "Change passwords") to prevent further compromise.
 

Phase 2: Incident Response (IR) Team Mobilization

Once the initial "fire" is contained, a formal team must be assembled to manage the crisis.

Assemble the Multi-Disciplinary Team: Form a group including IT, Security, Management, Legal, PR, and HR.
Identify and Analyze: * Determine the specific type of attack (Ransomware, Phishing, etc.).
Investigate the root cause and origin.
Re-analysis: If new signs of attack appear, return to the investigation phase immediately.
Document and Evidence Collection: Meticulously log the attack timeline and all response actions. Collect evidence for potential law enforcement or legal use.
Asset Repair: Evaluate damage and fix the specific weaknesses or vulnerabilities exploited by the attackers.
 

Phase 3: Communication and Compliance

Managing the external and legal impact of the breach.

Regulatory Compliance: Ensure adherence to data breach notification laws (e.g., meeting the GDPR 72-hour reporting window).
Inform Law Enforcement: Report the incident to the relevant authorities to assist in investigation and potential apprehension.
Stakeholder & Public Disclosure: * Issue a public statement if customer data is affected.
Be transparent regarding how the issue is being addressed to maintain trust.
Legal Preparation: Prepare for potential lawsuits, regulatory fines, or other legal repercussions.
 

Phase 4: Recovery and Future Hardening

Turning the incident into a defensive advantage.

Accountability: Identify responsible parties, including investigating potential internal threats.
Learn and Improve: Conduct a "post-mortem" analysis to strengthen defenses and update security protocols.
Consider Alternative Security Tools: If current software failed or persists in being ineffective, explore alternative antivirus or EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) programs.
 

Pro Tip: Documentation is your best friend during an audit or lawsuit. Ensure every timestamp, person involved, and action taken is recorded in a centralized, secure log.

 

 

 

Employee Emergency Checklist: In Case of a Cyberattack
 

If your computer behaves strangely (e.g., files disappearing, ransom notes appearing, or mouse moving on its own), stop what you are doing and follow these steps:

 

1. Immediate Isolation

Disconnect from the Internet: Turn off Wi-Fi and unplug any Ethernet (network) cables immediately.
Do Not Power Off: Unless instructed otherwise, leave the computer on but "Locked." Powering off can sometimes destroy volatile evidence stored in the RAM that the IR team needs.
Unplug External Drives: Remove USB sticks, external hard drives, or backup drives connected to your machine.
 

2. Immediate Reporting

Alert IT/Security: Call the IT Emergency Hotline or notify your manager immediately. Do not use your work email to report the issue if you suspect your account is compromised; use a phone or a separate, secure device.
Warn Your Teammates: Send a quick message (via a separate platform like Slack or a phone call) telling colleagues not to open any emails or links sent from your account.
 

3. Incident Documentation

While waiting for IT to respond, jot down exactly what happened:

What was the trigger? (e.g., "I clicked a link in an email," or "I downloaded a PDF.")
What were the symptoms? (e.g., "The screen went black," "A popup appeared," "My password was rejected.")
Note the Time: Exactly when did you first notice the irregularity?
 

4. Critical "Don'ts"

❌ DO NOT try to "fix" it yourself or run unauthorized scans.
❌ DO NOT communicate about the attack on public social media.
❌ DO NOT delete any files or "Cleanup" your browser history.
❌ DO NOT click "OK" or "Cancel" on suspicious pop-up windows; just leave them as they are for IT to see.
 

5. Follow-Up Actions

Update Passwords: Once IT gives the "all clear" from a different, clean device, change your corporate passwords and enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) if it wasn't already active.
Stay Alert: Be extra cautious of "follow-up" phishing attempts where attackers pretend to be IT staff "helping" you fix the issue.
 

Quick Note: Quick reporting is more important than being "right." It is always better to report a false alarm than to ignore a real attack.
