# How to Disagree Well in Security Engineering: The Power of Framing

> In cybersecurity, your technical expertise gets attention, but your communication skills build trust, influence decisions, and drive security outcomes.

Security engineers regularly disagree with developers, architects, operations teams, business leaders, vendors, auditors, and management. The difference between a productive discussion and a difficult conflict often comes down to **how you frame the conversation**.

This is especially important in **oil & gas, energy, utilities, manufacturing, and other critical infrastructure industries**, where security decisions affect safety, compliance, operational technology (OT), business continuity, and regulatory requirements.

---

# Why Disagreeing Well Matters

Security teams are often responsible for saying:

- No to insecure designs
- No to risky exceptions
- No to bypassing security controls
- No to unsupported technologies

However, constantly saying **"No"** creates resistance.

Instead, successful security professionals learn to:

- Build trust
- Encourage collaboration
- Focus on business outcomes
- Explain risk objectively
- Guide teams toward secure decisions

Your goal is **not to win arguments**—it is to improve security while maintaining strong working relationships.

---

# 1. Don't Say: "We need to talk."

### Instead say:

> **"I'd like to share some observations and get your perspective."**

### Why it works

This approach:

- Reduces immediate anxiety or defensiveness
- Shows respect
- Invites a two-way discussion
- Encourages collaboration instead of confrontation

### Security Engineering Example

Instead of:

> "We need to talk about your insecure deployment."

Say:

> "I'd like to share some observations about the deployment and understand your team's perspective before we discuss possible improvements."

---

# 2. Don't Say: "The problem is..."

### Instead say:

> **"Here's how I understand the current situation."**

### Why it works

This:

- Avoids assigning blame
- Creates room for different viewpoints
- Demonstrates curiosity
- Encourages constructive dialogue

### Security Engineering Example

Instead of:

> "The problem is your application has poor security."

Say:

> "Here's how I understand the current architecture. I believe there are a few areas where we can reduce security risk."

---

# 3. Don't Say: "You always..." or "You never..."

### Instead say:

> **"I've noticed recently that..."**

### Why it works

This:

- Focuses on observable behaviour
- Avoids personal criticism
- Keeps the discussion fact-based
- Makes the feedback easier to accept

### Security Engineering Example

Instead of:

> "You never follow secure coding practices."

Say:

> "I've noticed recently that several pull requests have bypassed the security review process."

---

# 4. Don't Say: "I don't even know where to start."

### Instead say:

> **"My goal for this conversation is..."**

### Why it works

This:

- Establishes a constructive tone
- Gives the discussion direction
- Helps everyone focus on outcomes

### Security Engineering Example

> "My goal for this discussion is to identify a solution that satisfies both our security requirements and your delivery timeline."

---

# 5. Don't Say: "You made me feel..."

### Instead say:

> **"When this situation happened, I felt..."**

### Why it works

This:

- Takes ownership of your emotions
- Connects feelings to specific events
- Avoids blaming individuals
- Encourages empathy

### Security Engineering Example

Instead of:

> "You ignored the security team's advice."

Say:

> "When the production deployment proceeded without the agreed security review, I was concerned because it increased operational risk."

---

# 6. Don't Rush to Fill Every Silence

### Instead say:

> **"Let's take a moment to think about this."**

or

> **"Let's come back to this after we've had time to review the details."**

### Why it works

Silence gives people time to:

- Process information
- Consider alternatives
- Reduce emotional reactions
- Make better decisions

### Security Engineering Example

During a risk acceptance meeting:

> "This decision impacts our regulatory compliance. Let's take a few minutes to review the evidence before deciding."

---

# 7. Don't Push for Immediate Solutions

### Instead ask:

> **"What options do you see moving forward?"**

### Why it works

This:

- Encourages ownership
- Promotes collaboration
- Builds stronger solutions
- Reduces resistance

### Security Engineering Example

Instead of:

> "You need to fix this by Friday."

Ask:

> "What implementation options would allow us to reduce the risk while meeting your release schedule?"

---

# 8. Don't End the Discussion Vaguely

### Instead say:

> **"Let's agree on the next steps and schedule a follow-up."**

### Why it works

This creates:

- Accountability
- Clear ownership
- Defined timelines
- Better execution

### Security Engineering Example

> "Let's document the agreed remediation actions, assign owners, and review progress during next Tuesday's security governance meeting."

# Applying This in Oil & Energy Companies

Security engineers in the oil, gas, and energy sectors frequently work with:

- OT/ICS engineers
- SCADA teams
- Plant operations
- Field engineers
- Cloud and infrastructure teams
- Enterprise architects
- Developers
- Network engineers
- Compliance and audit teams
- Executive leadership
- Third-party vendors

These environments demand collaboration because security decisions can affect:

- Operational continuity
- Personnel safety
- Environmental protection
- Regulatory compliance
- Critical infrastructure resilience
- Production availability

Respectful communication builds trust, making it easier to introduce security improvements without disrupting operations.

---

# Practical Security Conversations

## Security Design Review

Instead of:

> "Your architecture is insecure."

Say:

> "I'd like to understand the design decisions so we can identify opportunities to reduce security risk together."

---

## Vulnerability Management

Instead of:

> "You haven't patched your servers."

Say:

> "I've noticed several critical vulnerabilities remain open. Can we discuss what's preventing remediation and how we can help?"

---

## Cloud Security

Instead of:

> "You deployed this incorrectly."

Say:

> "Here's what I observed in the Azure environment. Let's review whether there's a more secure implementation."

---

## Incident Response

Instead of:

> "Your team caused the incident."

Say:

> "Let's review what happened and identify improvements that strengthen our overall security posture."

---

## Risk Exception Requests

Instead of:

> "Security won't approve this."

Say:

> "Let's explore options that reduce the business risk while supporting your operational requirements."

---

# Key Takeaways

Effective security professionals don't just protect systems—they build influence through communication.

When disagreements arise:

- Lead with curiosity rather than assumptions.
- Focus on facts, not personalities.
- Describe situations instead of assigning blame.
- Invite collaboration rather than imposing solutions.
- Give people time to think.
- Agree on clear actions and follow-up.
- Align security decisions with business objectives.

The strongest security engineers are trusted advisors who can challenge ideas respectfully, influence stakeholders, and guide teams toward better security outcomes. In critical industries such as oil, gas, and energy, the ability to **disagree well** is just as valuable as technical expertise.
